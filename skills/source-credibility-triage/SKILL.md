# Source Credibility Triage

## When to use
A source you have never heard of is about to carry a conclusion.

## Steps
1. Leave the page first. Search the site or organisation name with words like "funding", "criticism", "correction", "who runs", and read what independent parties say. The About page is self-description.
2. Identify the accountable party: a named author, editor or legal entity, with a way to reach them.
3. Establish interest: who pays, what they sell, and whether the conclusion benefits them. An interested source can be right, but needs independent support.
4. Check expertise against this specific claim. A credential in one field does not transfer to another.
5. Look for method: data, links and limitations you could follow and test.
6. Check track record: visible corrections, retractions, documented fabrication, imitation of a known outlet's name or domain.
7. Assign a use level and say why: rely on it; use for leads only; quote only as that party's position; or exclude.

## Watch out for
- Polished design, a .org domain and a long reference list prove little. Open two or three references and see whether they support the text.
- A high search ranking is not an endorsement.
- Do not reduce this to a politics label. A source can have a slant and accurate facts, or look neutral and invent them.
- Official bodies are authoritative about their own rules and publications, not automatically about every empirical question.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
Each load-bearing source has a stated use level with one concrete reason found outside the source itself.
