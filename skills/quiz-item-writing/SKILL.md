# Quiz Item Writing

## When to use
Questions are needed to check learning, written from known material.

## Steps
1. Get the objectives, source material, audience, purpose (practice or graded by the person setting it) and length.
2. Make a blueprint: how many items per objective, and how many require recall, application or judgement.
3. For each item, write the point being tested before the question.
4. Put the whole problem in the stem and avoid negatives; if unavoidable, make them prominent.
5. Build multiple-choice distractors from real misconceptions, all plausible and parallel in length and grammar. Three good options beat five with filler.
6. Avoid "all of the above", trick wording and clues from one item to another.
7. For short answers, write acceptable variants and what earns partial credit.
8. Record the key with a source reference and one-line feedback per wrong option.
9. Answer every item cold: is exactly one answer defensible, and can someone guess it without knowing the material?
10. Vary answer positions and check language for cultural or reading-level barriers unrelated to the skill.

## Watch out for
- The longest, most qualified option is usually right when writers are careless.
- Testing trivia from footnotes measures memorisation of the wrong things.
- If asked to answer a quiz someone is currently taking for credit, decline; this skill is for authoring and study.
- After use, items nearly everyone misses may be badly written.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
Every item maps to an objective, has one verified answer with a source, and wrong options have feedback.
