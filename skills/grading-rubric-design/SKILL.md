# Grading Rubric Design

## When to use
Open-ended work must be judged fairly and explained to those assessed.

## Steps
1. Read the task and objectives. Every criterion must trace to something taught and requested.
2. Choose analytic (separate criteria) for feedback, or holistic for quick overall judgement.
3. Limit criteria to what can be judged independently, usually three to six, so one flaw is not penalised twice.
4. Define three or four levels by observable features. Replace "good analysis" with what the work does.
5. Describe the top and the passing level first, then the others.
6. Set weights to reflect the objectives, and check that polish cannot outweigh substance.
7. Decide gates, late and missing parts separately from quality, following the institution's policy.
8. Score samples across the quality range, or invented ones. Where two markers differ by a level, tighten the wording.
9. Rewrite a learner-facing version and share it before the work starts.
10. The instructor makes and records the actual grades.

## Watch out for
- Counting features (three sources, five paragraphs) rewards compliance over quality.
- Adding points can give a total that contradicts holistic judgement; test extreme combinations.
- Do not tailor criteria after seeing particular students' work to move their grades.
- If asked to predict a grade for someone's own submission, give criterion-based feedback and say the assessor decides.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
Sample scoring produces consistent results, weights match objectives and each level is described in observable terms.
