# Reading Notes

## When to use
A text is available, or the reader has highlights, and wants notes worth returning to.

## Steps
1. Ask why they are reading it: apply, cite, discuss, study or enjoy. That sets depth.
2. Establish what you actually have: full text, some chapters, highlights or only a title. State coverage at the top. Do not summarise from memory as if you had read the file.
3. Record title, author, year, edition or URL and access date.
4. State the central question and the author's answer in two or three sentences.
5. Capture key ideas by section in your own words, each with its support (data, example, argument) and a location. Use only as many as the text earns.
6. Copy quotes character for character with page, section or e-book location. Mark omissions and edits. If a location is unknown, say so rather than guess.
7. Label three layers visibly: author says, quoted, my view.
8. Note weak support, assumptions, disagreements, terms to define and sources to follow.
9. Finish with what this changes for the reader: actions, links to other notes, and two or three questions to test recall later.

## Watch out for
- Paraphrase that stays so close to the original that it later gets reused as one's own words.
- Page numbers that differ between editions and e-books.
- For research papers, copying the abstract's claims without checking results and limitations.
- Long extracts of copyrighted text; keep quotes short.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
Coverage is stated, ideas and quotes have locations or are marked unlocated, and the reader's views cannot be mistaken for the author's.
