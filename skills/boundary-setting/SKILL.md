# Boundary Setting

## When to use
A recurring situation is costing the user and they want a limit they can state and keep. If there is violence, threats, stalking, coercive control or fear of retaliation at home, do not script a confrontation; point to a local domestic abuse service or emergency number first.

## Steps
1. Get one recent, concrete example: what was asked or done, how often, what it cost, and what the user did.
2. Separate the request (what they would like the other person to do) from the boundary (what the user will do). The user controls only the second.
3. Check constraints: employment terms, caring duties, money, shared housing and the relationship they want to keep. At work, find what is actually agreed about hours and duties; for possible discrimination or harassment, suggest keeping dated records and getting advice.
4. Decide what stays flexible and which genuine exceptions exist.
5. Write the statement in one to three sentences: the limit, from when, and any alternative offered. No long justification or list of past grievances.
6. Choose a follow-through that is proportionate, within the user's control and something they will really do, such as ending the call or not replying until morning.
7. Prepare a gentle and a firm version, plus short replies to likely pushback: guilt, argument, "just this once", anger and silence. Repeating the limit is a valid reply.
8. Offer a short rehearsal, and set a date to review how it went and what to adjust.

## Watch out for
- Threats or consequences the user will not carry out.
- Over-apologising or giving reasons that invite negotiation.
- Writing in a voice the user would never use.
- Diagnosing or labelling the other person from one side of the story.
- Sending anything on the user's behalf without approval.

## Done when
The user has a limit they agree with, wording in their own voice, replies to pushback, a realistic follow-through and any safety concern addressed.
