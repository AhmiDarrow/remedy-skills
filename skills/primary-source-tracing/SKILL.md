# Primary Source Tracing

## When to use
A figure, finding or announcement appears everywhere, and the user needs to know what was originally said.

## Steps
1. Pick the most distinctive fragment of the claim: an odd number, an unusual phrase, a named report.
2. Open several articles and note whom each one cites. Draw the chain: A cites B, B cites C. Follow the actual links.
3. Sort by publication date, then check for edits: an "updated" stamp means the visible text may be newer than the first date.
4. When a chain ends at "a study found" with no link, search the author, institution and year, or the distinctive number plus `filetype:pdf`.
5. In the original, locate the exact page, table or paragraph. Record the sentence around the number, including its population, time window and unit.
6. Compare original and popular version for dropped qualifiers: "up to", "among respondents", "projected", "in mice", relative versus absolute change.
7. Report the chain, the origin, and where the wording drifted.

## Watch out for
- Twenty outlets copying one wire story are one source, not twenty.
- Circular citation happens: an encyclopedia entry cites an article that took the line from that entry. Check which came first using archived copies.
- A press release is not the study. It often states the result more strongly than the paper does.
- If the original is paywalled, use the abstract or a legitimately free author copy and say exactly what you could not read.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
You can point to the originating document and location, or state clearly where the trail stops and that the claim is unsourced beyond that point.
