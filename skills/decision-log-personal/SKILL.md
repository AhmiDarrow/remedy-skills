# Decision Log Personal

## When to use
A choice matters enough that the user will later ask why they made it, or they keep going back and forth.

## Steps
1. Write the decision as a question with a date it must be made by. Note whether it can be reversed, and at what cost; easy reversals deserve less analysis.
2. List real options, including doing nothing, delaying, and a small trial. If there are only two, look for a third.
3. Separate must-haves, which eliminate options, from preferences. Ask the user to rank preferences before scoring so the weights are not bent toward a favourite.
4. For each option record the evidence, the unknowns, and the most plausible way it goes wrong ("it is a year later and this failed; why?").
5. Ask which single unknown could change the answer and whether it can be checked cheaply before the deadline.
6. Record the choice in the user's words, the main reason, and what they are knowingly giving up.
7. Write two or three predictions that can be checked, each with a rough probability, plus the signal that would make them change course.
8. Save as a dated entry in the file they choose and give the review date. At review, compare predictions with what happened and judge the reasoning separately from the luck.

## Watch out for
- Writing the entry after the outcome is known; label it as a reconstruction, since memory shifts toward what happened.
- Weighted totals that hide a gut preference. If the total feels wrong, find the missing criterion.
- Choosing for the user. Lay out trade-offs.
- Medical, legal or large financial decisions: organise the questions and say which professional should advise.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
A dated entry holds the options, reason, accepted downside, checkable predictions, change-course signal and review date.
