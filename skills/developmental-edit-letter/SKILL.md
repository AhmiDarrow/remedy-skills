# Developmental Edit Letter

## When to use
A long draft is complete enough to judge and the writer wants to know what to fix first.

## Steps
1. Ask what the piece is meant to do, for whom, and which stage it is at. Ask what they already know is broken.
2. Read it through once without commenting, noting only where attention dropped or confusion started, with the location.
3. Make a reverse outline: one line per section or scene stating what it does. Note word count for each.
4. Compare the outline with the stated aim. Find sections doing no work, doing the same work, or in the wrong order, and promises made early that never pay off.
5. For argument pieces, test each claim for support. For stories, track what the protagonist wants and what changes in each scene.
6. Write the letter: what works (with locations), then at most three to five problems ranked by how much else depends on them.
7. For each problem give the evidence, the effect on a reader, and one or two possible approaches.
8. Suggest a revision order, structural moves before anything sentence-level.

## Watch out for
- Line-editing passages that may be cut by the structural fix.
- Rewriting the work toward your own taste or a different genre.
- Listing twenty equal-weight notes; the writer cannot tell which matter.
- Vague verdicts like "the middle drags" with no scene named.

## Operating rules
- Look before you change anything: read the real files, data or page first.
- Report what you actually ran and saw; never invent results, sources or numbers.
- Ask before destructive, paid, public or irreversible actions.
- Never print, store or commit secrets or private personal data.

## Done when
The writer has a reverse outline and a ranked list of problems, each tied to specific locations, with a stated order of attack.
